The Middle Manager Is Becoming an Endangered Species

Companies are removing management layers. The work those managers performed has not necessarily gone away.

A company announces a flatter organizational structure. Several management positions disappear. Senior leaders describe the change as a way to accelerate decisions, reduce bureaucracy, and bring employees closer to the people setting strategy.

The announcement sounds promising. Fewer approval levels can make an organization easier to navigate. Employees may gain more independence. Leaders may become more accessible. And the company can reduce management expenses.

But six months later, employees are unsure who can resolve conflicts between teams. Senior executives are spending more time on routine operational decisions. New supervisors have larger groups to manage and less time to develop people. The company has fewer managers, yet many of the activities those managers performed remain.

The question is not simply whether an organization has too many managers. It is whether it understands what management contributes before deciding how much of it to remove.

Why the middle is an easy target

Middle management has long occupied an uncomfortable position. Senior executives want faster execution. Employees want fewer unnecessary approvals. When work moves slowly, the management layer between them is an obvious place to look.

There are legitimate reasons to reconsider these roles. Some managers spend too much time preparing reports that nobody uses. Others supervise work that employees could manage independently. In certain organizations, multiple levels review the same decision without improving it.

Technology has also changed what a manager needs to do. Dashboards provide information that once had to travel through several people. Collaboration platforms allow employees to coordinate directly. AI can assist with scheduling, reporting, analysis, and other administrative tasks.

None of that automatically eliminates the need for management. It changes the case for particular management activities. Removing an unnecessary approval step is not the same as eliminating the person responsible for resolving competing priorities, developing employees, or making sure decisions become operational reality.

The work that does not appear on an organization chart

A manager's formal responsibilities may include supervising employees, reviewing performance, approving schedules, and meeting departmental goals. Much of the work that keeps an organization functioning happens between those responsibilities.

A manager notices that two departments are interpreting a new policy differently and helps them resolve it before customers are affected. Another recognizes that a promising employee is struggling with a new responsibility and provides coaching before performance declines. Someone else knows why an earlier initiative failed and prevents a team from repeating the same mistake.

These activities are difficult to capture in a position description or productivity report. Their value often lies in problems that never become visible because someone addressed them early.

That does not mean every middle-management position is indispensable. It means a responsible redesign must account for the less visible work as well as the obvious tasks.

When fewer managers means more management work

Organizations sometimes assume that eliminating management positions eliminates the work attached to them. More often, that work moves.

A senior leader inherits additional direct reports and becomes involved in questions that were previously handled elsewhere. Experienced employees become unofficial team coordinators without the authority or time to manage competing demands. Supervisors oversee larger groups while retaining the same operational responsibilities.

The costs may appear as slower decisions, inconsistent treatment of employees, delayed feedback, or increased turnover. Managers with very large teams may have little time for the conversations that help employees improve or prepare for greater responsibility.

An organization can reasonably decide that managers should supervise more people. But the number of direct reports is only one consideration. The complexity of the work, employees' experience, geographic distribution, regulatory requirements, and amount of coordination needed all affect what a manager can realistically handle.

A team of experienced professionals performing independent work may need a different management arrangement from a team of new employees working in a high-risk operation. Applying the same span-of-control target to both can create very different results.

AI changes the argument, but does not settle it

The introduction of AI gives organizations another reason to reconsider managerial roles. If software can produce status summaries, identify workflow delays, or answer routine procedural questions, some activities that once occupied managers' time may become less necessary.

That creates an opportunity to redesign jobs. A manager who spends fewer hours compiling reports might spend more time addressing performance issues, developing employees, or improving how work moves between functions.

It also creates a temptation to count every automated task as evidence that a management position can be eliminated.

A system can flag an employee's missed deadlines. It cannot independently determine every relevant circumstance, repair a damaged working relationship, or assume responsibility for a difficult personnel decision. Data can reveal a bottleneck without resolving the competing interests that created it.

Organizations should evaluate what technology actually replaces, what it assists, and what remains a human responsibility. Those are different categories of work, and confusing them can lead to poor decisions.

The risk to the next generation of leaders

There is another consequence that may take years to become apparent. Middle-management positions have traditionally provided opportunities for employees to learn how to lead others, manage resources, handle conflict, and translate organizational priorities into day-to-day decisions.

If organizations eliminate those opportunities without creating alternatives, they may reduce their future pool of experienced leaders.

A high-performing individual contributor does not automatically know how to supervise a team. Nor does an executive become effective simply by receiving a more senior title. Leadership responsibilities require practice, feedback, judgment, and exposure to decisions with consequences.

Organizations can develop those skills through project leadership, rotational assignments, mentoring, and other experiences. But those alternatives need to be intentional. Eliminating positions today without considering how tomorrow's leaders will develop is a decision with a delayed cost.

A better question than how many managers to cut

Before removing a management layer, an organization should examine how decisions are made and how work actually moves.

Which approvals add value, and which exist because nobody has reconsidered them? Where do managers resolve problems that employees cannot address on their own? Which responsibilities could be automated or reassigned without reducing service or accountability? Who will coach employees, manage performance, and develop successors after the redesign?

The answers may support eliminating certain positions, combining others, or creating new roles with different responsibilities. They may also reveal that the problem is not the number of managers but poorly defined authority, duplicative processes, or an operating model that requires too many handoffs.

The objective should be a better-functioning organization, not simply a smaller management population.

The middle may change more than it disappears

There is no reason to preserve every management position simply because it exists. Organizations evolve, and some roles should evolve or disappear with them.

But management is not only a layer on an organizational chart. It is a collection of responsibilities that connect decisions to execution and employees to the organization they work for.

Companies can remove a layer with a restructuring announcement. They cannot remove the need for those responsibilities by announcing that the organization is now flatter.

The future of middle management may involve fewer administrative tasks, different spans of responsibility, and more emphasis on coaching, judgment, and cross-functional coordination. That is a meaningful change.

Whether it becomes a meaningful improvement depends on what organizations choose to redesign, what they choose to eliminate, and whether they understand the difference.
