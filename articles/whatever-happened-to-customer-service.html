Customer Experience + Business Operations · Nicole L. Turner

Whatever Happened to Customer Service?

Customers have more ways to reach a company than ever before. Getting someone to actually solve a problem can still feel impossible.

The package was delivered to the wrong address. The customer has a photograph showing a front door that is not hers. She contacts the retailer, explains what happened, and receives a link to the shipping company. The shipping company directs her to the retailer. The retailer's chatbot asks for the order number, then provides the same delivery confirmation she has already seen.

Forty minutes later, she knows exactly as much as she did when she started: someone has her package, and nobody seems responsible for finding it.

It is an ordinary customer service experience, which may be the most troubling part.

Businesses have invested heavily in customer relationship systems, mobile apps, automated messaging, self-service portals, and increasingly sophisticated artificial intelligence. Customers can initiate contact at almost any hour. They can track a request, receive a case number, and sometimes watch the status change in real time.

Yet the fundamental promise of customer service remains remarkably simple: when something goes wrong, someone should help make it right.

Access is not the same as assistance

Organizations often measure how easily customers can enter a service process. They track response times, the number of calls answered, the percentage of inquiries handled through self-service, and the volume of tickets closed.

Those measures can be useful. None necessarily tells a business whether the customer's problem was solved.

A chatbot can respond in two seconds without answering the question. A call center can meet its average handling-time target by transferring complicated cases elsewhere. A ticket can be marked resolved because the customer stopped replying, even though the original issue remains.

From the company's point of view, the process may be operating as designed. From the customer's point of view, the company has made it difficult to get help.

This difference becomes more pronounced when businesses design service around reducing contact rather than resolving the reasons customers make contact in the first place.

How did the customer become the coordinator?

Think about how much work customers are now expected to perform. They search frequently asked questions, navigate automated menus, create accounts, reset passwords, upload photographs, repeat identifying information, explain the same issue to multiple representatives, and monitor requests for updates.

Some of these steps are reasonable. Many customers prefer to handle straightforward matters themselves, and a well-designed self-service option can be faster than waiting for an employee.

But there is a difference between giving customers a convenient choice and transferring the company's administrative burden to them.

Consider a billing error involving a subscription. The customer contacts support and is told to speak with the billing department. Billing explains that the charge originated with a third-party processor. The processor says only the subscription company can authorize a refund. The customer returns to support and starts again.

Each department may have followed its own procedures correctly. The failure lies in the way the organization handles an issue that crosses departmental boundaries.

The customer has effectively become the project manager for a problem the company created.

What gets rewarded gets repeated

Customer service problems are not always caused by employees who are unwilling or unable to help. Sometimes the organization makes effective service difficult.

Representatives may be evaluated on call duration, adherence to scripts, the number of cases processed, or the percentage of contacts redirected to automated channels. They may have limited authority to issue refunds, correct account errors, or depart from a prescribed workflow.

Imagine an employee who could resolve a customer complaint in ten minutes but would need managerial approval to do so. Instead, the employee must transfer the case, create a record, and wait for a decision. The additional steps protect a process, but they may also increase the cost of the interaction and frustrate the customer.

When employees are rewarded for moving people through a system rather than addressing what brought them there, the system can become highly efficient at producing unsatisfactory experiences.

It is tempting to call this a training problem. Training may help, but it cannot give employees authority they do not have, correct conflicting policies, or connect systems that do not share information.

Technology can help, but it cannot own the outcome

Artificial intelligence introduces another opportunity to improve service. It can summarize customer histories, retrieve information, route inquiries, translate conversations, and help employees respond more quickly. For routine questions, automation may be exactly what the customer wants.

The risk comes when organizations treat the ability to automate an interaction as proof that the interaction should be automated.

A customer checking store hours has a different need from someone disputing a significant charge, reporting a safety concern, or trying to resolve a mistake that has already survived three previous contacts.

Good service design accounts for those differences. It allows customers to reach a person when judgment, discretion, or accountability is required. It also ensures that the person has enough information and authority to do something useful.

Otherwise, technology simply creates a faster way to encounter the same organizational obstacles.

The costs do not end with an unhappy customer

Poor service is often discussed as a reputation problem, but its operational consequences deserve equal attention.

Every repeat call consumes employee time. Every unnecessary transfer adds work. Every unresolved error can produce more messages, escalations, refunds, disputes, and exceptions. Employees spend time managing the consequences of a problem instead of preventing its recurrence.

Customers also make decisions. Some tolerate the inconvenience because switching providers is difficult. Others reduce their spending, warn friends, or leave when a viable alternative appears.

Businesses may not immediately connect those outcomes to the original service failure. The service department records a completed interaction. Finance records a refund. Sales records a lost customer. Operations records additional workload. Each function sees its own result.

Without examining the entire experience, the organization may never see the cost of making customers work so hard to get assistance.

Start with the problem the customer actually brought you

Improving customer service does not necessarily require a new platform, a larger call center, or a campaign reminding employees to be courteous.

It may require following a real customer problem from the first contact to the final outcome. Where does information get lost? Which decisions require unnecessary approval? How many times must the customer explain the issue? What happens when a case crosses functions? Who is responsible for making sure it is actually resolved?

Those answers can reveal problems that service metrics alone miss.

Businesses should also distinguish between customers who prefer self-service and customers who have been forced into it. A high rate of digital interactions may reflect convenience, or it may reflect how difficult the company has made it to reach anyone.

And when a recurring issue generates thousands of contacts, the best service improvement may be to fix the product, policy, billing process, or communication that creates those contacts.

The goal should not be to make complaints disappear from the dashboard. It should be to eliminate preventable problems and resolve the remaining ones without making customers carry the burden.

Customer service was never simply about being pleasant on the telephone. It was about a business taking responsibility for the experience it creates.

Customers do not need every interaction to feel extraordinary. Most would be satisfied with something much less ambitious: an answer that addresses the question, a person who can make a decision, and a company that follows through.

For all the advances in how businesses communicate with customers, that expectation has not changed.
