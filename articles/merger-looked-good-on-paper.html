Mergers + Business Performance · Nicole L. Turner

The Merger Looked Good on Paper

The financial case was convincing. The trouble began when two businesses had to become one.

The announcement described a compelling future: a larger customer base, complementary services, fewer duplicated expenses, and more opportunities for growth. Investors heard about efficiencies. Employees heard about possibilities. The presentation made the combined company look almost inevitable.

Then Monday morning arrived. A customer called the wrong service number. Two managers approved the same purchase using different rules. Sales teams disagreed about which accounts belonged to whom. Employees who had been promised a better organization could not get an answer about who made decisions anymore.

The merger had closed. The business had not yet been combined.

The spreadsheet was not wrong. It was incomplete.

A merger model can estimate overlapping leases, technology contracts, procurement savings, and potential revenue. Those calculations matter. But the model often assumes that eliminating duplication is as straightforward as identifying it.

Consider two companies that each have a customer support operation. Combining them appears to create an obvious savings opportunity. Yet one team resolves complicated problems through direct relationships with specialists, while the other routes every request through a centralized ticket system. The staffing totals are comparable. The way the work gets done is not.

Reducing positions before understanding those differences can remove the very practices customers value. Keeping both systems indefinitely, on the other hand, preserves cost and confusion. Neither outcome appears in the original savings estimate.

The financial case tells leaders what they hope to gain. It does not, by itself, explain what must change for those gains to become real.

Two organizations bring two sets of unwritten rules

Every organization develops habits that are rarely documented. How quickly can someone make a decision? When is a problem escalated? Who must be consulted before a commitment is made? Is an exception treated as good judgment or a violation of procedure?

These rules become visible when employees from different companies are expected to work together. A leader accustomed to making a decision in a ten-minute conversation may now need three approvals. Another employee may interpret a request for a meeting as unnecessary bureaucracy when a colleague considers it basic professional courtesy.

The disagreements can look personal. Often they are the predictable result of different operating histories.

Declaring that the combined company will adopt the 'best of both' sounds reasonable. It becomes meaningful only when someone examines the actual work, determines which practices produce better results, and explains why certain practices will continue while others will end.

The people who built the deal may not be the people who must deliver it

Transactions typically involve executives, financial advisers, attorneys, and a relatively small group of senior decision-makers. Once the agreement is signed, thousands of practical decisions move to managers and employees who may have had little involvement in shaping the deal.

They must reconcile policies, serve customers, retain employees, learn unfamiliar systems, and maintain daily performance while the organization is changing around them. Integration is often described as an additional project. For many employees, it becomes a second job.

This is where the gap between ambition and execution widens. Leaders announce a target date for combining operations, but the people responsible have not been given enough authority, time, or information to do it well. Departments optimize their own transitions without recognizing the consequences for other departments.

An integration office can track deadlines and decisions. It cannot substitute for understanding whether the redesigned work actually functions. Completion percentages may rise while customer complaints, rework, and employee frustration rise with them.

Customers do not experience a merger as a strategy

A customer does not care that two organizations expect to save money by consolidating their billing platforms. The customer cares that the invoice is correct, the service is reliable, and someone can fix a problem when it occurs.

A longtime client may have chosen one company because of its responsiveness or specialized knowledge. After the acquisition, that client is directed to a generic intake process, assigned a new representative, or asked to explain its history again. From the company's viewpoint, these are temporary integration issues. From the customer's viewpoint, the product has changed.

The damage may be gradual. Clients tolerate one mistake, then another. They begin exploring alternatives long before a renewal decision or cancellation appears in a report.

Integration measures should therefore include more than savings captured and systems retired. Complaint patterns, repeat contacts, service recovery, employee handoffs, and the reasons customers leave can reveal whether the combined company is delivering the value it promised.

The hardest decisions are not always the most expensive

A merger creates questions that cannot be resolved by choosing the lower-cost option. Which company's approach to quality should prevail? Should local managers retain authority or should decisions move to headquarters? What happens when a profitable customer segment conflicts with the combined company's new service model?

Postponing these decisions may feel diplomatic. It also allows parallel practices to become permanent. People continue using familiar methods, sometimes because no one has explained what replaces them. Over time, the company pays for the old organization and the new one simultaneously.

Leaders need a disciplined way to distinguish three categories: practices worth preserving, practices that must change, and practices that should be designed from scratch. The answer should follow evidence about performance and customer needs, not the relative prestige of the companies involved.

This also requires admitting when an early assumption was mistaken. A deal can remain worthwhile even when a planned integration choice needs to be reversed. Defending the original plan at all costs is not evidence of effective leadership.

What should happen before the celebration

The most useful integration questions begin before the transaction closes. Where do the two companies perform similar work differently? Which processes depend on particular individuals? What promises have customers been given? Which decisions will have to be made immediately, and who will have authority to make them?

Leaders should identify the operational dependencies behind major savings targets. If the model assumes a single technology platform, what must be true about data quality, workflows, training, security, and customer continuity before one platform can be retired? If a combined sales organization is expected to increase revenue, how will account ownership, incentives, and customer relationships work?

Some answers will remain uncertain until the organizations begin operating together. That is a reason to test assumptions early, not a reason to treat the model as a finished plan.

The transaction is a milestone. Integration is a sustained change in how people make decisions and deliver work. It deserves its own resources, its own measures, and permission to adjust when the evidence changes.

A successful deal is not the same as a successful business

The most persuasive merger presentation can show what two companies might become. It cannot guarantee that employees will know how to work together, that customers will receive better service, or that promised savings will survive contact with everyday operations.

Those results come from the less glamorous work of examining processes, resolving competing assumptions, making difficult choices, and checking what happens after the changes are introduced.

A merger can make sense on paper and still disappoint in practice. The question is whether leaders treat the paper as the destination or as the beginning of the work.
