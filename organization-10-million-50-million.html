Growth + Organizational Effectiveness · Nicole L. Turner

The Organization You Built for $10 Million May Not Be the Organization You Need for $50 Million

Growth doesn't just make an organization bigger. It changes what the organization needs to be able to do.

Reaching a major growth milestone can validate years of decisions. The company found a market. Customers responded. Revenue grew. More people were hired. New products, services, locations, or markets followed. Whatever the exact path, enough worked to move the business from one stage to another.

Success, however, can create an assumption that deserves closer examination: If this is what got us here, we need more of it to get us there.

Sometimes that is true. Sometimes the organization reaches a point where the way it has operated becomes less effective as the business around it becomes larger and more complex.

A $10 million company might function extremely well with a small group of people involved in most major decisions. Employees may work across loosely defined roles. Leaders may stay close to customers and day-to-day operations. Information can move quickly because the people who need it know one another. When something unusual happens, everyone knows who has the answer.

Those ways of working can be an advantage. They can make a company fast, responsive, entrepreneurial, and remarkably good at getting things done.

Then the company grows.

There are more customers, employees, decisions, products, locations, systems, managers, and dependencies between functions. People who once worked directly with one another now operate through teams. Decisions that used to require a conversation between two people affect six departments. Employees hired last month do not have the institutional knowledge of employees who have been there for ten years. Leaders have responsibilities that did not exist when their roles were created.

The business may have grown considerably while the organization supporting it has changed much less.

Growth introduces a different kind of complexity

Headcount is one of the easiest signs of growth to see, but it can be one of the least revealing. An organization can double in size without doubling its ability to coordinate work, make decisions, develop leaders, share information, or respond to problems.

In fact, growth often exposes dependencies that were easy to overlook when the organization was smaller.

Consider a company where most significant customer issues eventually find their way to one executive. At a smaller scale, that executive's involvement might be one reason the company is known for exceptional service. The leader knows the customers, understands the business, and can resolve an issue quickly.

As the company grows, the same practice can produce a different result. More issues reach the executive. Managers wait for decisions. Employees hesitate to act without approval. The executive becomes increasingly involved in operational matters while also being expected to focus on the future of the business.

Nothing about the original practice was necessarily wrong. The context changed.

The same thing happens with roles. An employee who was hired to perform a particular function gradually becomes the person who knows how several unrelated things work. A manager who once supervised five people is now leading managers who supervise 40. A founder who could personally communicate a new priority to everyone now has to rely on other leaders to interpret and carry that priority through the organization.

Growth changes the demands placed on people and systems, even when nobody formally redesigns them.

Success can conceal what the organization has outgrown

Organizations tend to scrutinize themselves when something is going badly. Declining revenue, customer complaints, missed targets, turnover, or other visible performance issues create urgency.

Growth can have the opposite effect. As long as the numbers continue moving in the right direction, there is less reason to question how much effort is required to produce them.

That can make success surprisingly good at hiding organizational strain.

Employees create workarounds when existing processes no longer fit the work. High performers absorb responsibilities because something needs to get done. Leaders intervene when decision authority is unclear. Teams create their own tools because enterprise systems do not meet their needs. Meetings multiply because people need another way to exchange information that no longer travels naturally through the organization.

Each response solves an immediate need, which is precisely why these practices can remain in place for years.

Eventually, however, an organization can become dependent on extraordinary effort to produce what appears to be ordinary performance.

That distinction matters. A company that consistently meets its commitments because a handful of experienced people know how to navigate around its limitations is in a very different position from a company whose normal way of operating reliably produces the same result.

The financial statements may not reveal the difference immediately.

The people doing the work usually can.

Scaling requires choices about what should change and what should not

The answer is not to replace everything informal with policies, processes, approval levels, and management layers. Growing organizations can become less effective when they mistake bureaucracy for maturity.

Some of what helped build the company may be worth protecting.

A leader's accessibility may remain a competitive advantage. Cross-functional flexibility may be part of why the company responds to customers so well. An informal practice may work better than the formal process designed to replace it. Speed may matter more than standardization in parts of the business.

The challenge is deciding which practices remain valuable at the next stage and which ones now limit it.

That requires looking beyond whether something still technically works. A better question is what the organization has to do to make it work.

Does a process function because it is well designed, or because experienced employees know how to work around it? Are managers actually making decisions, or are they carrying recommendations up the organization for approval? Has a role evolved along with the business, or has five years of additional responsibility simply been added to the original job? Can important work continue when a particular person is unavailable? Can employees get the information they need without knowing whom to call personally?

These are not questions reserved for organizations in trouble. They become more important precisely when a business is succeeding.

A growing organization does not need to abandon what made it successful. It does need to distinguish between practices that are part of its advantage and practices that have survived because capable people continue compensating for them.

There is also no universal point at which an organization needs to change. The $10 million and $50 million figures are illustrative. For one company, the inflection point might come with geographic expansion. For another, it might be the transition from one product to several, the addition of a second layer of management, an acquisition, rapid workforce growth, new regulatory requirements, or the founder stepping away from daily operations.

Revenue does not determine organizational complexity by itself.

What matters is whether the organization still fits the business it is being asked to support.

The organization that got a company to its current stage deserves credit for doing exactly that. But past success should not give every structure, role, process, decision practice, or way of working permanent status.

At some point, growth stops asking only whether the business can sell more, hire more, or serve more customers.

It begins asking whether the organization can carry what the business has become.
