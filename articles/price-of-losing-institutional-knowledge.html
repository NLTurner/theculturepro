Institutional Knowledge + Organizational Effectiveness · Nicole L. Turner

The Price of Losing Someone Who Knows How Everything Works

When a longtime employee leaves, an organization can lose far more than a position. It can lose the knowledge that makes everyday work possible.

The retirement announcement was expected. The disruption that followed was not.

For 22 years, one employee had been the person colleagues called when a payment failed, a vendor disputed an invoice, or a system produced a result nobody could explain. Her position description covered purchasing and administrative operations. Her actual contribution reached into nearly every part of the organization.

Her replacement inherited the job title, a set of procedures, and access to the same software. What the replacement did not inherit was the history behind countless decisions, the relationships that made problems easier to resolve, or the judgment developed through years of seeing what happened when things went wrong.

Within weeks, ordinary tasks were taking longer. Nothing dramatic had failed. The organization had simply lost a source of knowledge it had never fully recognized as an operating resource.

The job description tells only part of the story

Organizations are generally better at documenting positions than documenting how work actually gets done. A job description can identify responsibilities, reporting relationships, and required skills. It rarely captures the unwritten rules, exceptions, personal connections, and past decisions that make those responsibilities manageable.

An experienced employee may know which supplier needs an early reminder, which report contains an unreliable field, and why a seemingly unnecessary approval step was introduced five years ago. They may know that two systems use the same term to mean different things, or that a process works only because another department performs an informal check.

Much of this knowledge is acquired through repetition, mistakes, conversations, and exposure to unusual situations. It becomes so familiar to the employee that they may not think to explain it. Their colleagues may not realize how frequently they depend on it.

When the employee leaves, the organization discovers that the documented process was not the entire process.

The cost rarely appears under turnover

The obvious expenses of losing an employee include recruiting, onboarding, and the time needed for someone new to become productive. Those expenses matter, but they can understate the operational consequences.

Consider what happens when an employee who manages a complex client relationship leaves. A replacement may have the necessary technical skills but not know the history of previous commitments. A question that once took ten minutes now requires several meetings. A customer who was accustomed to a reliable point of contact begins receiving conflicting answers.

Elsewhere, coworkers spend hours reconstructing procedures, searching old messages, or contacting former employees. Supervisors become involved in decisions they previously delegated. Work is repeated because nobody knows why a particular approach was rejected years earlier.

None of those hours necessarily appears in a report labeled 'cost of turnover.' They are distributed across departments and budgets. Yet the organization is paying for the loss through slower execution, avoidable errors, and time diverted from other responsibilities.

The person who knows everything may also be a warning sign

It is tempting to describe the departing employee as irreplaceable. That may be a tribute to the person's contribution, but it can also reveal a problem in how the organization operates.

If only one person understands a critical process, the organization has created a dependency. If coworkers must know the right individual to get a question answered, access to information depends on relationships rather than a reliable system. If an essential task cannot continue during a vacation, the risk existed long before anyone submitted a resignation.

These arrangements often develop for understandable reasons. Experienced employees solve problems quickly. Colleagues learn whom to call. Managers value people who can make things happen without extensive supervision. Over time, the organization begins treating exceptional individual knowledge as a substitute for dependable operating practices.

The employee is not the problem. The problem is that the organization has allowed essential work to depend on knowledge it has not made accessible to others.

Why the handoff meeting is not enough

Many organizations respond to a planned departure by scheduling a few knowledge-transfer meetings and requesting written procedures. That is better than doing nothing, but it often produces a collection of instructions rather than an understanding of the work.

A checklist can explain how to submit a purchase order. It may not explain how to recognize a questionable request, which exceptions require escalation, or what to do when the approved process conflicts with an urgent operational need.

Effective transfer requires observing work in context. What questions does the employee answer repeatedly? Which problems are referred to them even though those problems fall outside their formal responsibilities? Which decisions require judgment? What would stop moving if they were unavailable for a month?

The answers can reveal relationships and dependencies that no standard operating procedure captures. They also identify which knowledge should be documented, which decisions should be redesigned, and which employees need experience before the departure occurs.

Retaining knowledge is not the same as retaining every practice

There is an important distinction between preserving what people know and preserving everything they do.

A longtime employee may have developed workarounds because the organization's systems were inadequate. Some of those workarounds are ingenious. Others have become unnecessary or introduce risk. Transferring them without examination can reproduce the very problems the organization should address.

The departure of an experienced employee can therefore be an opportunity to understand the work more fully. Why is this step necessary? Who relies on its output? What information is missing? Could the process be simplified, automated, or reassigned?

The goal should not be to create a perfect imitation of the person leaving. It should be to retain what the organization needs while improving the way the work is performed.

Build continuity before the farewell lunch

Organizations do not need to wait for a retirement announcement or resignation to identify where knowledge is concentrated. Managers can begin by examining recurring dependencies: tasks only one person performs, questions that always go to the same employee, client relationships with no backup, and processes that routinely require informal intervention.

Cross-training can help, but it must involve meaningful practice rather than simply giving someone access to a shared folder. Employees need opportunities to handle real situations, make decisions, and learn why the work is done a particular way.

Organizations should also make room for experienced employees to explain the exceptions, history, and tradeoffs that are absent from formal instructions. These conversations are more useful when they occur over time, while there is still an opportunity to test whether someone else can perform the work.

And leaders should examine whether the organization has rewarded people for being the only ones who know how to solve a problem. A workplace that depends on indispensable individuals may unintentionally discourage the sharing of knowledge that would make the entire operation more dependable.

What leaves when someone walks out

An employee's departure is often described as the loss of talent. Sometimes it is also the loss of an informal operating system: a network of relationships, decisions, historical knowledge, and practical judgment that held work together.

The organization may fill the vacancy quickly and still spend months discovering everything that left with the person.

The better question is not whether someone can be replaced. People move on, retire, change careers, and pursue new opportunities. Organizations should expect that.

The question is whether essential work can continue without depending on one person's memory, relationships, and willingness to keep rescuing a process that nobody else fully understands.

By the time the farewell card is signed, it may be too late to find out.
