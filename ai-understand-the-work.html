AI + Organizational Effectiveness · Nicole L. Turner

AI is Changing the Work, but Do We Understand the Work We're Changing?

Organizations are spending enormous amounts of money figuring out what AI can do. There is another question they need to answer first: Do we actually understand how work gets done here?

Organizations are spending enormous amounts of money figuring out what AI can do.

I think there is another question they need to answer first:

Do we actually understand how work gets done here?

Not how the process is documented.

Not what the organizational chart suggests.

Not what the policy says should happen.

How work actually moves through the organization.

Who has to approve what. Where decisions get stuck. Which employees have created workarounds just to get things done. Which reports are produced because someone requested them years ago and no one has questioned them since. Where information gets entered twice. Which meetings exist because the systems do not communicate. Which employees have become unofficial bridges between departments because the formal process does not work.

That is the organization AI is walking into.

And if leaders don't understand it, they may be asking AI to make a poorly designed organization move faster.

AI Adoption Is Not the Same as AI Transformation

There is no question that AI has entered the workplace.

McKinsey's 2025 global survey found that nearly nine in ten respondents said their organizations were regularly using AI. Yet most organizations still had not embedded it deeply enough into workflows to produce material enterprise-level benefits.

BCG found an even more striking gap. In its 2025 study of more than 1,250 companies worldwide, only 5% were generating AI value at scale. Sixty percent reported minimal revenue and cost gains despite significant investment.

That should tell us something.

The technology may not be the only thing standing between organizations and the value they expect from AI.

The organization itself may be standing in the way.

We have a tendency to talk about AI adoption as though the technology enters a neutral environment. It doesn't.

It enters organizations with histories.

It enters workflows that accumulated one decision at a time.

It enters departments that may not communicate particularly well with one another.

It enters processes containing approvals that once made sense but may no longer serve a purpose.

It enters jobs that have gradually absorbed responsibilities nobody intentionally designed.

It enters systems built around old technologies, old assumptions, old customer expectations, and sometimes old problems.

Then we ask a new technology to operate inside all of it.

Before You Automate the Work, Understand the Work

Imagine a process that requires seven approvals.

AI might help prepare the information needed for those approvals faster.

It might summarize the supporting documents.

It might identify missing information.

It might route the request to the appropriate people.

That's useful.

But there is another possibility worth considering:

Maybe the process never needed seven approvals.

If nobody asks that question, AI can make an unnecessarily complicated process more efficient without making the organization meaningfully better.

That distinction matters.

There is a difference between using AI to perform the existing work faster and using AI as an opportunity to reconsider why the work exists in its current form.

The second requires considerably more organizational understanding.

It requires leaders to look beyond tasks and examine the chain of decisions, relationships, dependencies, behaviors, incentives, and assumptions that produces the work.

McKinsey's research offers an important clue. Among 25 organizational attributes it studied in 2025, redesigning workflows had the greatest effect on whether organizations saw EBIT impact from generative AI. Yet only 21% of respondents using generative AI said their organizations had fundamentally redesigned at least some workflows.

In other words, many organizations may be introducing twenty-first-century technology into twentieth-century ways of working.

The Official Process and the Real Process Are Often Two Different Things

This is where I think leaders need to become especially curious.

Ask someone at the top of an organization how a particular process works, and you may hear one version.

Ask the person who performs that process every day, and you may hear another.

That difference matters.

Employees know which steps they skip because they add no value.

They know who actually has the answer when the process says someone else should.

They know which spreadsheet exists because the primary system cannot produce what they need.

They know which approval is largely ceremonial.

They know which customer problem repeatedly occurs because two departments own different pieces of the same experience.

They know where they have to send an email, make a phone call, update a system, and then send another email simply to move one piece of work forward.

Those aren't small operational details.

They are intelligence about how the organization actually functions.

And they become extremely important when deciding where AI belongs.

One of the mistakes organizations can make is designing their AI strategy from too far away from the work.

Executives understand strategy.

Technology teams understand systems.

Consultants may understand AI.

But the people doing the work often understand the friction.

You need all of those perspectives.

Because deciding what AI can do is a technology question.

Deciding what AI should do is an organizational question.

AI May Make a Bad Process Less Painful Without Making It Better

This is the part I think organizations should watch carefully.

Efficiency can disguise dysfunction.

If something that once took four hours now takes twenty minutes, everyone understandably celebrates the improvement.

But what if the activity itself contributes very little?

What if the information being produced is rarely used?

What if the approval exists because of a problem the organization solved five years ago?

What if three departments are performing variations of the same work?

What if employees spend hours reconciling information because two systems were never designed to work together?

AI may reduce the pain enough that nobody feels urgency to address the underlying design.

And that creates an unusual risk:

We could become exceptionally efficient at doing work we should have stopped doing.

That is not transformation.

It is faster execution of the existing organization.

AI Should Force a Different Conversation About Work

I don't think the most valuable AI conversations begin with:

Where can we use AI?

I think they begin further upstream:

What outcome are we trying to produce?

How does work currently move from intention to outcome?

Where does judgment actually matter?

Where does information get lost?

Where have we created complexity that no longer earns its keep?

What are people doing because the organization requires it rather than because the outcome requires it?

If we were designing this work today, knowing what AI can now do, would we design it this way at all?

Those questions lead somewhere very different.

They move the conversation from AI adoption to organizational design.

And that may be where much of AI's unrealized value is sitting.

Recent BCG research found that high-performing companies are roughly seven times more likely to redesign workflows and reshape the business end-to-end with AI. Nearly nine in ten CEOs in its 2026 study reported some cost or revenue benefit from AI in targeted areas, but most were still struggling to scale those gains across the enterprise.

That gap between using AI somewhere and changing how the organization creates value is worth paying attention to.

The Bigger Opportunity Isn't Automation

We are still early enough in this shift that many organizations are understandably focused on tools, pilots, policies, training, and use cases.

Those things matter.

But I suspect the organizations that ultimately gain the most from AI will not simply be the ones that adopted the best technology first.

They will be the ones willing to let the technology provoke harder questions about themselves.

Why does this process work this way?

Why does this job contain these responsibilities?

Why does this decision require these people?

Why does this information travel through five places before reaching the person who needs it?

Why are highly capable employees spending time moving information rather than interpreting it?

Why are we automating this instead of eliminating it?

And perhaps the most consequential question:

If we weren't already organized this way, would we build the company this way now?

AI can generate, analyze, predict, summarize, automate, and increasingly act.

What it cannot do for leaders is remove the responsibility to understand the organization they are asking it to improve.

Before AI can fundamentally change how work gets done, somebody has to be willing to see how work is getting done now.

And sometimes, what you see changes the problem you thought you were trying to solve.
