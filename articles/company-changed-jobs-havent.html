The Company Has Changed. The Jobs Haven’t.

New technology, new customers and new strategies often arrive faster than the responsibilities assigned to employees.
By Nicole L. Turner · The Culture Pro®

The business moved. The work stayed put.

A company expands into new markets, introduces digital services, changes its customer model, and invests in technology. Its strategy presentations describe a different organization from the one that existed five years ago. Yet employees arrive each morning to jobs designed for that earlier company. Their titles, decision rights, performance measures, and reporting relationships remain largely untouched.

This disconnect rarely announces itself as a single crisis. Instead, it appears in daily frustrations: a customer request that crosses three departments, a new system that creates extra manual work, a manager who must approve decisions that should be made elsewhere, or a team judged by a measure that no longer reflects what the business needs.

Leaders may conclude that employees are resisting change or failing to execute. But sometimes the organization has changed what it wants to accomplish without changing how the work is organized to accomplish it.

How yesterday’s jobs survive tomorrow’s strategy

Job design tends to receive attention at predictable moments: when someone is hired, when a position becomes vacant, or when a reorganization changes reporting lines. Business strategy, meanwhile, can evolve continuously. New products arrive, customer expectations shift, regulations change, and technology makes some activities unnecessary while creating entirely new ones.

Organizations often respond by adding duties rather than reconsidering the job itself. A service representative is asked to manage digital inquiries alongside phone calls. An analyst inherits another dashboard. An operations manager becomes responsible for a new platform but has no authority to change the process it supports. The job grows, yet its purpose and boundaries remain unexamined.

After several rounds of additions, the organization may be operating with roles assembled from historical decisions. Some employees carry responsibilities that no longer contribute much value. Others perform essential work that is absent from any formal description. Both situations can exist on the same team.

A different business requires different work

Consider a hypothetical regional service company that once depended on in-person transactions. It now serves customers through an online portal, offers subscription services, and competes across several states. The business model has changed considerably.

Its customer-service jobs, however, still emphasize processing transactions and answering routine questions. Today, many routine transactions happen automatically. The harder work involves resolving exceptions, explaining digital processes, recognizing recurring customer problems, and coordinating with technical teams. If the organization continues to hire, train, and evaluate employees for the old job, it should not be surprised when the customer experience disappoints.

The same problem appears in other functions. Finance may be expected to advise on subscription economics while spending most of its time producing reports built for one-time sales. Operations may be measured on the volume of completed tasks rather than reliability across an interconnected service system. Supervisors may retain layers of approval designed for a smaller, slower business.

None of these examples necessarily calls for more employees. They call for examining whether the organization is asking people to deliver new results through outdated assignments.

The hidden price of the mismatch

One cost is delay. Work that has no clear owner moves from meeting to meeting while employees seek permission or negotiate who should act. A second is duplication. Two teams may collect the same information because their responsibilities were never reconsidered after a technology change. A third is employee exhaustion. People spend time maintaining old processes while improvising the new ones the business actually needs.

There is also a cost to decision-making. A role may carry accountability for an outcome without authority over the choices that determine it. For example, a customer-experience manager may be expected to improve resolution times but lack the ability to change policies, staffing patterns, or system workflows. The organization then evaluates a person for a result the job was never designed to control.

These mismatches can be mistaken for performance problems. Training might help an employee use a new tool, but it cannot resolve contradictory responsibilities. A motivational message cannot compensate for a process that routes every decision through the wrong person. And hiring a new manager may simply add another layer to an already confusing arrangement.

Start with the work, not the organizational chart

When leaders recognize the problem, their first instinct may be to redraw reporting relationships or rewrite job descriptions. Those documents matter, but they should follow a more basic examination: What work must happen now for the business to deliver what it promises?

That examination begins with the current business model, not the legacy department structure. Identify the activities that create value for customers, the decisions required to complete them, the handoffs between teams, and the information people need. Then compare that reality with what employees are assigned and measured to do.

Look for work that has disappeared but remains in a role, new work that everyone assumes someone else owns, duplicated approvals, and responsibilities that depend on informal favors. Ask employees where they regularly compensate for a process that no longer fits. Their answers often reveal more about the actual operating model than a formal organization chart.

Only then should leaders decide whether to redesign a process, change a job, shift decision authority, build a skill, or create a new position. Sometimes the right answer is a smaller, more focused role. Sometimes it is a broader one with greater authority. The point is to design for the business that exists today.

Performance measures can preserve an obsolete job

Even when responsibilities change, old measures can pull employees back toward yesterday’s priorities. A team told to provide thoughtful solutions may still be rewarded primarily for how many calls it closes. A manager asked to improve collaboration may still be evaluated only on the output of their own department. A technology-enabled operation may continue counting manual transactions long after those transactions have ceased to be meaningful indicators of performance.

Employees respond to what the organization actually rewards. If the company announces a new strategy while retaining measures tied to the old one, the contradiction is built into the job. Updating a title without changing expectations and evaluation criteria leaves that contradiction intact.

What leaders should be able to answer

A useful test is whether leadership can explain, in practical terms, how a major business change altered the work. When the company entered a new market, which decisions moved closer to customers? When technology automated a process, what became the new priority for the people who previously handled it? When the service model changed, which jobs were redesigned to support the experience the company now promises?

If the answers are limited to new software, training sessions, and revised strategic goals, the organization may have changed its tools and ambitions without changing its operating habits.

People can adapt remarkably well, and many organizations depend on that adaptability. But informal workarounds are not a sustainable substitute for deliberate job design. A company cannot expect employees to deliver the future indefinitely through jobs built for its past.
