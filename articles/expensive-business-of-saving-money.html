Business Decisions + Organizational Effectiveness · Nicole L. Turner

The Expensive Business of Saving Money

The savings are easy to calculate. The consequences can be much harder to find.

A company eliminates a position and saves $95,000 in salary and benefits. A government agency consolidates contracts and reports a 12 percent reduction in spending. A nonprofit suspends professional development to protect its operating budget. A manufacturer reduces equipment maintenance expenses to improve margins.

Each decision produces a number that can be reported as savings.

But what happens afterward?

The eliminated employee’s responsibilities still need to be performed. The consolidated contract may require more coordination from internal staff. Managers who were supposed to receive training continue making decisions without the knowledge they needed. Equipment that could have been serviced during scheduled downtime eventually fails during production.

The expense disappeared from one part of the budget. The cost of the decision did not necessarily disappear with it.

This is one of the more consequential problems in organizational decision-making: the people responsible for delivering savings are not always the people who absorb their consequences.

The budget can improve while the business gets worse

Cost reduction is a legitimate business responsibility. Organizations cannot spend indefinitely, and leaders sometimes have to make difficult decisions to remain financially viable.

The problem arises when a reduction in spending is treated as sufficient evidence that the decision was successful.

Consider a customer service department that reduces staffing by 15 percent. Payroll expenses decline immediately. The savings appear in the next financial report.

Meanwhile, customers wait longer for assistance. Remaining employees handle more calls, resolve fewer issues on the first attempt, and spend additional time responding to complaints. Some customers stop calling altogether and take their business elsewhere.

The financial benefit is concentrated in payroll. The consequences are distributed across customer retention, employee turnover, productivity, and revenue.

Unless someone examines those results together, the organization may celebrate a successful cost reduction while other parts of the business are deteriorating.

What makes this particularly difficult is that the consequences rarely arrive at the same time as the savings. They may develop gradually, making it harder to connect an unfavorable result to an earlier decision.

The hidden expense of making fewer people do more

Reducing headcount without reducing the amount of work is one of the most familiar approaches to controlling expenses.

Sometimes organizations genuinely have redundant positions or work that no longer needs to be performed. Eliminating those costs can improve efficiency.

Other times, the work remains, and the organization simply redistributes it.

An employee leaves, and three colleagues inherit portions of the position. A manager assumes responsibilities previously handled by an administrative specialist. A technical employee spends several hours each week preparing reports that were once produced by a dedicated analyst.

The organization has fewer employees, but it has not necessarily become more efficient.

In fact, the arrangement may be more expensive when the additional work is performed by employees whose time costs more or whose primary responsibilities produce greater value.

There is also the matter of what stops getting done.

Employees cannot absorb unlimited responsibilities without making choices. Some tasks receive less attention. Others are delayed. Work that contributes to future performance is often sacrificed to meet immediate demands.

The organization may continue functioning, but functioning and performing effectively are not the same thing.

When purchasing decisions create operating expenses

The lowest-priced option is not always the least expensive.

Imagine an organization evaluating two software providers. One offers a lower annual subscription fee but requires extensive manual data entry, limited technical support, and additional work to connect with existing systems.

The second provider costs more but automates several tasks and reduces the time employees spend correcting errors.

If the purchasing decision considers only subscription fees, the first option wins.

If it considers implementation, employee time, maintenance, errors, service interruptions, and the useful life of the system, the result may be different.

The same problem appears in decisions involving facilities, equipment, contractors, technology, and outsourced services.

A lower contract price can produce higher internal labor costs. Deferred maintenance can lead to expensive repairs. Eliminating a support service can transfer responsibilities to employees who lack the time or expertise to perform them effectively.

Organizations often have detailed processes for approving expenditures. They may have much less effective processes for determining what those expenditures actually cost to use, maintain, or replace.

Some savings consume the future

Not every cost reduction produces an immediate operational problem.

Some decisions weaken an organization’s ability to perform months or years later.

Professional development is a useful example.

When budgets tighten, training is often treated as discretionary because its benefits may be less visible than the cost of delivering it.

Yet organizations continue promoting employees into management positions, introducing new technologies, expanding services, and expecting people to take on responsibilities that require knowledge they have not developed.

The consequences may eventually appear as poor decisions, ineffective supervision, preventable mistakes, employee departures, or failed initiatives.

By then, the original training reduction may be a distant memory.

A similar pattern occurs when organizations eliminate succession planning, reduce investments in technology, postpone process improvements, or allow essential expertise to become concentrated in a few individuals.

The immediate savings are measurable. The future exposure is less certain.

That uncertainty can make the reduction appear financially attractive even when the organization is accepting a substantial risk.

Why the true cost is so difficult to see

Part of the problem is how organizations divide responsibility.

Finance tracks expenditures. Human resources monitors staffing and turnover. Operations measures production and service delivery. Customer-facing teams monitor complaints, satisfaction, and retention.

These functions may each see part of a decision’s consequences without anyone examining the complete picture.

A finance team may accurately report that a department spent $400,000 less than the previous year. An operations team may accurately report that work is taking longer. A customer service team may accurately report an increase in complaints.

All three reports can be correct.

What may be missing is an examination of whether the results are connected.

There is another complication. Organizations sometimes measure the success of a cost-reduction initiative by whether the targeted amount was eliminated, rather than whether the decision improved the organization’s overall performance.

When that happens, achieving the savings target can become more important than understanding what the savings produced.

A better way to evaluate savings

Before making a reduction, leaders should understand the work, resources, and relationships affected by the decision.

That means examining more than the expense being eliminated.

For example, if a position is removed, what responsibilities will disappear with it? Which will remain? Who will perform them? What existing work will receive less attention? How will the organization know whether service, productivity, or quality has changed?

If a less expensive vendor is selected, what internal resources will be needed to manage the contract? How will performance be measured? What happens if the provider fails to deliver?

And if a program is suspended, what was the program intended to prevent, improve, or make possible? What evidence will indicate whether eliminating it created problems elsewhere?

These questions do not mean every expense should be protected. Some activities are no longer useful. Some processes are unnecessarily complicated. Some resources can be deployed more effectively.

But identifying waste requires understanding what an expenditure contributes, not simply identifying where money is being spent.

A reduction that eliminates unnecessary work is different from one that makes necessary work more difficult.

The number that matters is not always the number being reported

There are circumstances when organizations must reduce expenses quickly, even knowing that the decision will have consequences. Financial distress, declining revenue, and changes in market conditions can leave leaders with few attractive options.

Those situations make understanding the tradeoffs more important, not less.

An organization may reasonably decide to accept a temporary decline in service, postpone an investment, or redistribute responsibilities to protect its financial position. But leaders should know what they are accepting and monitor whether the consequences remain manageable.

The real danger is treating the reduction itself as the achievement.

A lower expense does not automatically mean a more efficient operation. A smaller workforce does not necessarily mean higher productivity. A cheaper contract does not guarantee better value.

Sometimes the most expensive decision an organization makes is the one that initially appears to save the most money.

And sometimes the first indication of that expense appears nowhere near the budget line where the savings were recorded.
